# Finding ideas people pay for: idea-finding methods, and the base rates for reaching about 10k per month of profit (as of 22 October 2026)

*Conventions: "(snippet)" marks a figure taken from a search-result summary whose page could not be opened or was not opened, so it is unverified. "Anecdote" marks a single founder's or seller's own account. USD and EUR are treated as interchangeable at the ~10k/month scale; all sources below report USD unless stated.*

## 1. The proven idea-finding and pre-validation methods: procedure, evidence used, strengths, failure modes, and which steps an AI agent could run

### Takeaway
None of the named practitioner methods (Sales Safari, The Mom Test, JTBD switch interviews, Million Dollar Weekend, etc.) has been tested in a controlled study that this research could find; the only randomized evidence is for the generic "scientific approach" (state hypotheses, test them, evaluate the results), which raised revenue and made founders pivot more often in an Italian startup trial (2020), and led to more terminated ideas in a 759-firm replication (2024). The practitioner methods agree on two points: start from what a reachable group already does or pays for, not from brainstormed ideas, and count only costly commitments (money, time, reputation) as validation. AI is useful for desk research, pain mining and coding interview transcripts, but every test found shows synthetic respondents cannot replace real buyers' commitments.

### Cited Findings

**What is actually tested (cross-method evidence)**
- RCT: Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella & Spina (Management Science, 2020) trained Italian startups (2016–17) to "articulate a theory, develop hypotheses, test them, and critically evaluate the results"; treated founders "earn more revenues" and "pivot to a greater extent" to a different idea; the news release gives "a hundred" startups and no effect sizes — [Bocconi news](https://www.unibocconi.it/en/news/entrepreneurs-who-think-scientists-get-better-results). Search-result summaries of the paper give 116 startups followed for about a year (snippet; paper not opened) — [paper PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2019-camuffo.pdf); [Bocconi IRIS record](https://iris.unibocconi.it/handle/11565/4013977).
- A 2024 replication in the Strategic Management Journal covered 759 firms in four field trials: more idea terminations among treated firms, and a non-linear pivot pattern (snippet; paper not opened, so its performance measures are unverified) — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/32437).
- Why validation matters: in a CB Insights analysis of 101 startup post-mortems, "no market need" was the most cited single cause (~42%) (snippet) — [CB Insights report, PDF hosted by Talentica](https://www.talentica.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-20-Reasons-Startups-Fail.pdf). A later count of 111 post-mortems put running out of cash or failing to raise first (38%) and no market need second (35%); these are founders' own explanations (snippet) — [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/startupsmart/analysis/startups-fail-founders-capital/).
- Paul Graham: "by far the most common mistake startups make is to solve problems no one has" — [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html).

**Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas" (November 2012)**
- Procedure: do not brainstorm; "Live in the future, then build what's missing"; notice what "seem[s] to be missing"; ask "what you wish someone else would build" and "What would you pay for right now?"; look at the "clash of domains"; mine needs found in your own previous work (Hotmail began as a tool its founders used to discuss an earlier startup idea while holding day jobs) — [PG essay](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html).
- Evidence it relies on: the founder's own experience ("organic" ideas that "grow naturally out of the founders' own experiences"), plus YC case examples (Stripe, Hotmail) — [PG essay](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html).
- Strengths: targets depth of need, "something a small number of people want a large amount" (a "well"), with the test "who wants this right now?"; names schlep blindness (Stripe's payments problem was skipped by programmers who "shrank from having to deal with payments") and the unsexy filter as hidden sources of good ideas — [PG essay](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html).
- Failure modes it names: made-up or "sitcom" ideas that "sound plausible enough to fool you"; polite encouragement ("Sum that reaction across the entire population, and you have zero users"); rationalising needs; filtering too early; giving competitors too much credit — [PG essay](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html).

**Sales Safari (Amy Hoy and Alex Hillman, 30x500)**
- Procedure: pick an audience you already belong to (peers, aspiring peers, clients) that has an online presence and buys on value; find its "watering holes" (forums, email lists, groups, blogs and comments, LinkedIn and Facebook groups, tool-support forums, Amazon-style review sections), places where it talks unprompted; record "Pain", "Jargon" and "Recommendations" with a link and date per observation; tag each comment with a painful emotion (a 30-item cheat sheet: anger, anxiety, avoidance, boredom ... wastefulness, worry) or painful action or inaction; note repeating patterns; then publish "Ebombs" ("The 'I get it' version of marketing") before building a product. Sales Safari is "the raw material ... not necessarily build a product" — [egghead.io, 30x500 "Sales Safari in a Nutshell"](https://egghead.io/learn/30x500/sales-safari).
- Evidence it relies on: unprompted, naturally occurring conversations (observation, not interviews), which avoids the interviewer effect; no observation quota is given — [egghead.io](https://egghead.io/learn/30x500/sales-safari).
- Strengths (Cedric Chin, Commoncog, March 2025): keeping demand analysis separate from solution design ("By separating these steps you are preventing yourself from lying to yourself"); works best on busy threads (Hoy's demo used a ~60-post thread) — [Commoncog forum](https://forum.commoncog.com/t/speedrunning-the-skill-of-demand-commoncog/2584).
- Failure modes reported: low-traffic forums yield "drive-by questions"; one pass is not enough to master it (Chin practised 30+ hours); watering-hole chatter skews to price-sensitive shoppers rather than premium buyers; low-interest categories have few watering holes; "if there's no demand, don't fight it" — [Commoncog forum](https://forum.commoncog.com/t/speedrunning-the-skill-of-demand-commoncog/2584).
- One forum member used "Deep Research" AI models to find new watering holes (anecdote) — [Commoncog forum](https://forum.commoncog.com/t/speedrunning-the-skill-of-demand-commoncog/2584).

**Rob Walling: Start Small, Stay Small (2010) and the stair-step approach (2015)**
- Procedure (book): market first: "Find people to buy your product before deciding what to build"; "Market comes first, marketing second, aesthetic third, and functionality a distant fourth"; prefer small niches that are cheap to advertise to, cluster in shared communities and are ignored by large competitors; "better to capture 100% of a 5,000-person niche than to capture only 10% of a 50,000-person niche" — [Michael Lynch's review of the book](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/start-small-stay-small).
- Weaknesses noted by the reviewer (2019): "Much of it feels dated"; many recommended tools are defunct; outsourcing advice is unrealistic — [mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/start-small-stay-small).
- Stair-step: step 1, a one-time-sale product, favouring add-ons sold in WordPress, Shopify or Heroku marketplaces because the marketplace supplies discovery; step 2, repeat until the income lets you drop other work; step 3, a recurring-revenue product, only after the first two because subscription revenue ramps slowly (snippet; Walling's original URL now returns 404) — [MicroConf repost](https://microconf.com/latest/the-stairstep-approach-to-bootstrapping); [secondary notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/sources/articles/the-stair-step-method-of-bootstrapping-by-rob-walling).

**The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)**
- Procedure: ask about past behaviour and specifics, not opinions or hypotheticals; treat compliments as no evidence; end every meeting by asking for a commitment or an advance. Commitment means the prospect gives up something they value; the book names three currencies: time, reputation risk and cash — [Goodreads quote page](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/11029920-commitment-can-be-cash-but-doesn-t-have-to-be-think) (snippet). Advancement means moving to the next step of your real sales process. Examples of real signals: paying, a letter of intent, handing over data, trying an unfinished build, an introduction to the decision-maker, a scheduled follow-up; meetings that end in praise or with no defined next step are red flags (snippet) — [Indie Hackers post relaying the book's definitions](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/did-that-customer-meeting-really-go-that-well-ed2b905ad3).
- Evidence it relies on: past behaviour and costly commitments obtained in conversation, not opinions about the future (snippet) — [Indie Hackers post](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/did-that-customer-meeting-really-go-that-well-ed2b905ad3).

**Jobs-to-be-done switch interviews (Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek; Demand-Side Sales 101, Moesta with Greg Engle, 2020)**
- Core claims: "If there is no struggle, there is no demand" (p. 193); "until there's a struggling moment, an accumulation of events, people don't buy" (p. 150); four forces: push (the ongoing problem) and pull or magnetism (attraction of the new option) must outweigh anxiety (doubt about the new option) and habit; "the forces work as a system", so added features can raise anxiety (p. 69) — [Jim Nielsen's book notes](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2020/book-notes-demand-side-sales/).
- Interview technique: refuse "like" as a reason ("'Like' is not a cause, it is an excuse for not knowing why", p. 103); use contrast (ask why not the alternative); anchor vague words ("There's no fast, only faster than...", p. 104) — [Jim Nielsen's notes](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2020/book-notes-demand-side-sales/).
- The switch interview reconstructs one real past purchase along a timeline from first thought through active searching to the decision, coded into the four forces (snippet) — [Young Urban Project guide](https://www.youngurbanproject.com/customer-interviews-for-positioning/); a recorded live switch interview (a mattress purchase) at a 37signals workshop with Moesta, Spiek and Jason Fried — [jobstobedone.org](https://jobstobedone.org/?p=383) (snippet).
- Cost: JTBD interviews "take at least an hour" (Cedric Chin) — [Commoncog forum](https://forum.commoncog.com/t/speedrunning-the-skill-of-demand-commoncog/2584).
- Warning: a vendor blog claims an "86% innovation success rate" for JTBD versus 17% for feature-driven approaches with no source; treat as unsupported (snippet) — [koji.so](https://www.koji.so/blog/jobs-to-be-done-interview-guide-2026).

**Arvid Kahl, The Embedded Entrepreneur (May 2021, 306 pages)**
- Structure: audience discovery, audience exploration, problem discovery, audience-building (snippet) — [LoveReading listing](https://www.lovereading.co.uk/audiobooks/author/arvid-kahl); [Bookrastinating](https://bookrastinating.com/book/108104).
- Approach: the reverse of building a product and then hunting for a problem; audience-first founders gather validation before the product is finished; technical founders often start coding too early (snippet) — [Indie Hackers recap](https://indiehackers.com/post/how-to-build-an-audience-driven-business-arvid-kahl-the-embedded-entrepreneur-32c13207fd).

**Justin Jackson, market-first ("Business is like surfing", 21 February 2020)**
- Thesis: "If business was surfing, the market would be the wave"; "Demand grows on its own", and positioning cannot manufacture it. A market is a group "spending money (or willing to spend money) on a given product category or solution" — [justinjackson.ca/surfing](https://justinjackson.ca/surfing).
- Procedure: build fundamentals (skills, relationships, watch what buyers purchase); score a market on current value (customers × spend × purchase frequency), growth rate, reach and competition; check personal fit; then decide what to build; differentiate within an existing category rather than inventing one — [justinjackson.ca/surfing](https://justinjackson.ca/surfing).
- Failure modes named: building before fundamentals; a wave too small or flat; creating a new category (April Dunford: "one of the hardest ways to get initial traction"); expecting certainty. Evidence is anecdotal (ConvertKit, a Shopify gallery plugin, his own podcast-hosting company) — [justinjackson.ca/surfing](https://justinjackson.ca/surfing). A March 2020 Indie Hackers episode set him against Tyler Tringas on choosing a market — [Indie Hackers #152](https://www.indiehackers.com/podcast/152-tyler-tringas-and-justin-jackson).

**Noah Kagan, Million Dollar Weekend (2024, with Tahl Raz)**
- Procedure: "NOW, Not How"; idea sources: your own annoyances, accessories or services around best-selling products, marketplace demand (Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace, completed eBay listings), AnswerThePublic, Google Trends, r/SomebodyMakeThis; a one-minute check (Google Trends direction flat or rising; audience size from the Facebook Ads tool); validation target "Find three customers in 48 hours who will give you money for your idea", via presales to your network, a marketplace listing, or ads to a landing page; offers framed as Price + Benefit + Time; follow up because many yeses come on the second or third attempt; scale "one customer, then ten, then a hundred" — [Graham Mann's book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/million-dollar-weekend-noah-kagan).
- Thresholds vary by source: three paying customers or ten prospects who say they intend to buy, within 48 hours (snippet) — [Silicon Hills News](https://www.siliconhillsnews.com/?p=7130); sales must be paid upfront, not promised (snippet) — [Wealthtender](https://wealthtender.com/insights/three-essential-takeaways-from-million-dollarweekend/).
- The "ask 100 people" element was not found; the book's "Law of 100" is a pledge to produce 100 outputs (emails, posts), and the rejection drill is the "Coffee Challenge" — [Graham Mann's notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/million-dollar-weekend-noah-kagan).

**Brian Casel, productized services**
- Definition: a done-for-you result at a fixed scope and price, delivered by a documented process ("a productized service is one that runs systematically") — [Startups for the Rest of Us ep. 208, 28 October 2014](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel). Casel and Dan Norris are credited with popularising the model (snippet) — [t2informatik](https://t2informatik.de/en/smartpedia/productized-service/).
- Procedure: pick one service clients have already paid for, ideally with measurable results; pick one customer niche with real budgets; mine recurring prospect questions for deal-breakers; price the outcome, not hours; offer a "ladder" (a one-time audit that upgrades to a monthly plan); standardise, streamline and document each process, then delegate — [SFTRFOU ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel).
- Evidence it relies on: demand already proven by paid client work. Anecdote: adding a setup fee cut signups but "The cancellation rate goes to near zero at that point" — [SFTRFOU ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel).
- Failure modes named: consulting creep ("This is not consulting"), the founder bottleneck ("If I just do it myself, I can get it done so much quicker"), selling work you have never delivered; documentation can take up to three times as long as doing the task — [SFTRFOU ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel).
- Anecdote: Casel's Audience Ops (productized content marketing, started 2015) sold in September 2021 for a disclosed "high six-figure" price; he argued productized services make a more sellable business than consulting (snippet) — [theygotacquired.com](https://theygotacquired.com/?p=208).

**Greg Isenberg and community- or Reddit-driven idea mining**
- Procedure (secondary recap, 30 August 2024, of his video "How To Find A Startup Idea"): browse subreddits of about 10,000–100,000 members (the "Goldilocks Zone") in a domain where you have a hobby or skill, using GummySearch; look for complaint threads, advice requests and direct asks for a fix; research what niche creators already cover (Perplexity, YouTube); sketch and mock up a product — [Substack recap](https://koenvanlysebetten.substack.com/p/from-reddit-to-riches-decoding-greg).
- Caveats in the recap: communities are "quick to call out imposters"; "If everyone's solving the problem, maybe the real problem is too many solutions"; no criteria for how many complaints count as demand; the 10k–100k range is unsourced — [Substack recap](https://koenvanlysebetten.substack.com/p/from-reddit-to-riches-decoding-greg).
- IdeaBrowser (co-founded by Isenberg, launched around May 2025) is a scored library of 1,000+ ideas drawn from Reddit, search and trend data; a competitor argues it suits finding ideas more than checking your own (snippets) — [Product listing](https://hunted.space/product/ideabrowser-com); [Preuve comparison](https://preuve.ai/compare/ideabrowser).

**"Boring business" and acquisition-first approaches**
- Codie Sanchez: buys cash-flowing local businesses (laundromats, car washes, ice vending) sourced through referrals, financed by seller notes, SBA-backed debt or revenue-share deals that she said let her buy with none of her own money (snippets; advocate's own account) — [My Wife Quit Her Job ep. 419](https://mywifequitherjob.com/episode419/); [James Altucher Show](https://jamesaltuchershow.com/episode/codie-sanchez-on-how-to-become-a-main-street-millionaire-the-secrets-to-profiting-from-boring-businesses-ysfo).
- Walker Deibel, Buy Then Build (2018): the publisher's pitch says most startups collapse or plateau, but gives no data; Deibel is linked to Quiet Light brokerage and an acquisition accelerator, so the framing is advocacy (snippets) — [Lioncrest](https://lioncrest.com/books/buy-then-build); [Scaling Up bio](https://scalingup.com/atlanta-summit/walker-deibel).

**Newer AI-assisted methods (2024–2026) and what is known about whether they work**
- GummySearch, the best-known Reddit audience-research tool (it "programmatically generates business ideas"), announced its closure on 6 November 2025 after failing to agree a commercial Data API licence with Reddit; sales ended 30 November 2025, full shutdown and data deletion 1 December 2026; "over 135,000 founders, marketers, and investors" had used it — [GummySearch, "The Final Chapter"](https://gummysearch.com/final-chapter).
- Nielsen Norman Group (Rosala and Moran, 21 June 2024) re-ran three of its real-participant studies with ChatGPT and the Synthetic Users product: answers were "too shallow"; synthetic learners claimed to finish every course while real learners often dropped out; "Since AI loves to please, every idea is often seen as a good one"; recommendation: use for desk research and preparing studies, never "for final decisions or idea validation" — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/synthetic-users/).
- Academic check (Bisbee et al., Political Analysis, 2024): about half of persona-based regression estimates differed significantly from the real survey results, and about a third of those reversed sign (snippet, relayed by a vendor of real-customer interviews) — [koji.so methodology page](https://www.koji.so/docs/synthetic-users-research-methodology).
- Positive result, narrow scope: "semantic similarity rating" (LLM free-text answers mapped to Likert scales by embedding similarity) reached "90% of human test-retest reliability" and KS similarity > 0.85 across 57 purchase-intent surveys (9,300 human responses) in personal-care products from one company; asking LLMs directly for numeric ratings gave unrealistic distributions — [arXiv 2510.08338, Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08338); coverage names PyMC Labs and Colgate-Palmolive — [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/llms-achieve-90-accuracy-in-replicating-consumer-purchase-intent/).
- HBS working paper 23-062 (Brand, Israeli, Ngwe, 2023): GPT answering as a shopper produced willingness-to-pay estimates consistent with demand theory (higher simulated incomes, higher price tolerance); its WTP for fluoride in toothpaste ($3.40) was close to a human study's ($3.27) (snippet) — [HBS PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/23-062_47458a4a-8be2-4b53-a3a3-9b5af20578b1.pdf); [MSI listing](https://www.msi.org/working-paper/using-gpt-for-market-research).

### Inferences
- **Comparative digest** (my synthesis of the findings above):

| Method | Core signal it trusts | Main strength | Main failure mode | Steps an AI agent could run | Steps that stay human |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PG "live in the future" | Founder's own lived need | Finds deep, early needs others miss (schleps) | Not a procedure; needs years at the leading edge; easy to rationalise | Mine the founder's own notes, tickets and complaints for "what would you pay for now"; list schleps in a domain | Living in the domain; judging depth of need |
| Sales Safari | Unprompted talk in watering holes | Avoids interviewer bias; yields buyer language for marketing | Slow; skews to vocal, price-sensitive posters; pain ≠ money | Find watering holes, collect posts with links and dates, tag pain/jargon/recommendations, cluster patterns | Judging which pains carry budgets; belonging to the audience |
| Walling market-first + stair-step | Existing spend, reachable niche | Fits part-timers: small one-time products first, discovery from marketplaces | Dated tactics; marketplace dependence | Size niches (search volumes, marketplace review counts, competitor pricing pages) | Choosing the niche and channel |
| The Mom Test | Past behaviour + costly commitment | Converts talk into evidence (time, reputation, cash) | Small samples; founders still misread politeness | Draft scripts; flag compliments, hypotheticals and future tense in transcripts; track commitments and next steps | Holding the conversations; asking for the commitment |
| JTBD switch interview | Reconstructed real purchase (forces, timeline) | Explains why people switch and what anxiety blocks it | 1-hour+ interviews; needs recent switchers; best for existing categories | Recruit recent switchers from reviews and forums; code transcripts into push/pull/anxiety/habit | Running the interview |
| Embedded Entrepreneur | Recurring problems inside an audience | Distribution exists before the product | Months of audience-building; an audience is not demand | Monitor communities for recurring questions | Earning trust over time |
| Justin Jackson market-first | Category spend and growth | Rides existing demand; avoids category creation | Pushes into crowded categories | Score markets on value, growth, reach, competition | Personal-fit judgment |
| Million Dollar Weekend | Cash from 3 buyers in 48 h | Fast, cheap, unambiguous money signal | Friends-and-network false positives; small-ticket bias | Build the landing page, offer and checkout; run the ad test; draft outreach | Making the asks |
| Productized service (Casel) | Clients already paying for the work | Revenue before software; proven demand | Consulting creep; founder bottleneck | Mine repeated client requests; draft fixed-scope offers and SOPs; automate parts of delivery | Delivery quality; sales calls |
| Isenberg/Reddit mining | Complaints and requests in mid-size subreddits | Cheap, fast candidate generation | Vocal minority; crowded ideas; data-access shutdowns | Scan subreddits, rank complaint clusters | Deciding what to test |
| Acquisition-first | Audited cash-flow history | Skips the zero-to-revenue risk | Capital, debt, inherited problems; advocates' bias | Screen listings against a buy box; compute multiples; check traffic and platform concentration | Diligence, negotiation, operating |

- The methods fall into three evidence tiers: (1) observing behaviour in the wild (Sales Safari, Reddit mining, Kagan's marketplace scans, Jackson's category spend); (2) interviews about past behaviour (Mom Test, JTBD); (3) commitment tests (Mom Test commitments, Kagan's three paying customers, selling a productized service before building software). Only tier 3 answers "will they pay"; tiers 1 and 2 find who hurts and why. A factory that wants "ideas people pay for" should treat tier 1–2 output as hypotheses and require a tier-3 signal before calling an idea validated, which is also what the RCT evidence rewards (explicit hypotheses, explicit tests).
- AI agents are strongest at tier 1 (search, collection, clustering, sizing) and at the analysis side of tier 2 (coding transcripts). The evidence says they must not stand in for tier 3: synthetic respondents are sycophantic (NN/g) and the one strong positive result (SSR) concerns ranking concepts in a mature consumer category, not whether a new B2B offer will be bought.
- The Reddit route carries platform risk for the factory itself: the leading tool closed over API licensing, so any agent that mines Reddit should use licensed or manual access.
- The sources pull in different directions: Kagan's speed (48 hours) against Hoy's patience (practice, Ebombs first), and Jackson's market-first against Kahl's audience-first. A part-time founder can reconcile them by using Jackson or Walling to pick the market, Sales Safari and the Mom Test to find the pain, and Kagan or Casel to get the money signal.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of any named method (Sales Safari, Mom Test, JTBD, Kagan) was found; their evidence is practitioner experience and case stories.
- The phrase "pain with money attached" could not be traced to Rob Walling; searches found only generic pain-and-payment frameworks.
- The Mom Test's chapter text, The Embedded Entrepreneur's chapter list, the exact JTBD timeline stage labels (passive looking, deciding, consuming) and Kagan's "ask 100 people" step were not verifiable from primary text in this session.
- No independent evaluation of AI idea-mining or idea-validation tools (IdeaBrowser and similar) was found; only vendor claims and competitor comparisons.
- The 2024 SMJ replication's revenue outcome and the 2020 trial's effect sizes were not read in the papers themselves.

## 2. Base rates: what the MicroConf State of Independent SaaS reports and other benchmark data say about small bootstrapped SaaS and productized services

### Takeaway
The newest MicroConf State of Independent SaaS report is still the 2024 edition (data collected late 2023, roughly 469–700 respondents); no 2025 or 2026 edition was found as of October 2026. Its picture is sobering: the largest single MRR bucket is under $1,000 MRR, about 30% of companies are flat or shrinking and another 30% grow only 1–4% a month, full-time-led companies grow 30% faster than part-time-led ones, and SEO and word of mouth are rated the highest-impact channels. Larger datasets agree: the median company that ever reached $1M ARR took 2 years 9 months to get there (ChartMogul, survivors only), bootstrapped private SaaS grows about 20% a year at the median (SaaS Capital), and an unverified third-party analysis of Stripe-verified indie revenue puts the share of products above $10k MRR at roughly 6%.

### Cited Findings

**MicroConf State of Independent SaaS: editions, sample and dating**
- Editions archived: 2020, 2021 and 2022 (report PDFs on Dropbox) and 2024; no 2023 entry — [MicroConf report page](https://microconf.com/state-of-indie-saas). The 2024 report is the fourth edition, "produced over roughly five years" — [Startups For the Rest of Us ep. 721, July 2024](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-721-7-key-takeaways-from-the-2024-state-of-independent-saas-report).
- Sample: "469 surveyed independent SaaS founders across all stages", "Data collected late 2023; refresh when the next report is released" (page updated June 2026) — [MicroConf $0–10K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr). Conflicting counts: "nearly 700 usable responses" from the MicroConf, TinySeed and podcast audiences — [SFTRFOU ep. 721](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-721-7-key-takeaways-from-the-2024-state-of-independent-saas-report); "surveyed 700 startups" — [MicroConf On Air episode page](https://microconf.com/on-air-episodes/7-surprising-saas-trends-every-founder-must-know-in-2024).
- The 2022 and 2021 report PDFs could not be retrieved (Dropbox links return error pages), and the 2021 episode page publishes no figures — [MicroConf 2021 episode](https://microconfpodcast.com/episodes/the-2021-state-of-independe-saas-report).

**MicroConf 2024: revenue distribution and growth**
- Under $1,000 MRR is "the biggest single bucket": 28% of companies (2024 report, p. 34) — [MicroConf $0–10K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr). A search snippet attributed 17.7% to the same p. 34 bucket on a MicroConf page; none of the five MicroConf pages fetched shows 17.7%, so the discrepancy is unresolved (snippet) — [MicroConf report page](https://microconf.com/saas-this-year).
- Customer counts: the page reports "65%" as the share of companies with 1–10 paying customers and says "More than half have under 50" (p. 25); the two statements are inconsistent as printed — [MicroConf $0–10K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr).
- Growth: "grow just 1–4% month over month, and another ~30% are flat or shrinking" (p. 35) — [MicroConf $10K–100K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/10k-100k-arr).

- Combining the base rates: among indie SaaS that already have revenue, reaching ~$14k MRR (≈10k/month profit at a 70% margin) is a top-decile outcome, roughly the top 5–10% if the unverified TrustMRR figure (~6% above $10k MRR) is close; MicroConf's sample, drawn from an engaged, conference-going audience, probably flatters the base rate.
- Time: ChartMogul's 2–2.5% median monthly growth would take years to compound to $14k MRR. From $1k MRR it takes ≈107 months at 2.5%/month, ≈55 months at 5%, ≈28 months at 10% and ≈19 months at 15% (ln(14.3)/ln(1+g)). The MicroConf average monthly growth figures (14%, 17%, 26%) are means over small bases and are not sustainable rates.
- Part-time: the only measured part-time effect is the 30% slower growth of part-time-led companies. A part-time founder should therefore plan with the slower curves, or pick models that need fewer customers (higher ARPA) so that slower growth still reaches the target.
- Pricing is the strongest controllable lever in the data: higher entry prices bring in ~3.4x more new MRR per month (MicroConf, p. 49) and much better retention (ChartMogul ARPA bands).
- Productized services reach revenue faster than SaaS (demand is pre-proven by paid work), but agency-style net margins (13–30%) mean ~33k–77k of monthly revenue for 10k of profit unless the founder delivers most of the work personally; a solo operator keeps a much larger share (Designjoy anecdote), at the cost of hours.

## 3. The arithmetic of about 10k per month net: price points, customer counts, churn, support load, and where self-serve gives way to sales-led

### Takeaway
At the ~70% profit margins small SaaS report on acquisition marketplaces, 10k/month net needs about 14k MRR: ~490 customers at $29/month, ~145 at $99, ~48 at $299, or ~14 at $999. The low-price paths are much harder than the counts suggest because churn, retention and support per dollar all get worse as price falls. The evidence places pure self-serve below roughly $1k–$5k a year per customer and sales-assisted selling above roughly $3k–$5k, so a part-time founder's best zone is a $100–$500/month B2B product that a self-serve or founder-led sale can close.

### Cited Findings
- Profit margin of small SaaS: Acquire.com SaaS listings averaged 67% (2023) and 71% (2024) margins, stable in 2025 — [Acquire.com Jan 2026 report](https://blog.acquire.com/acquire-com-biannual-acquisition-multiples-report-jan-2026/).
- Customer counts in practice: MicroConf reports that "more than half" of indie SaaS have under 50 paying customers (p. 25, as printed) — [MicroConf $0–10K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr).
- Churn by acquisition model (averages, period not stated, presumably monthly): freemium ~11%, card-required trial 5.5%, no-card trial 6.34% — [SFTRFOU ep. 721](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-721-7-key-takeaways-from-the-2024-state-of-independent-saas-report).
- Retention by price: top-quartile annual customer retention is 63.1% at ARPA under $10/month; 2.7% of such companies reach NRR > 100% versus 41.1% above $500/month; top-quartile GRR 60–70% under $50/month; B2B/B2C dividing line ~$25/month ARPA — [ChartMogul Retention Report](https://chartmogul.com/saas-retention-report/).
- Price and new revenue: $1,000+/month starting plans add ~$1,073 new MRR per month versus ~$316 for $1–9 plans — [MicroConf $10K–100K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/10k-100k-arr).
- Support load (practitioner experience, no counts): "Normalized by account it's (often) the little users, by lots ... Normalized by dollar value it isn't even funny"; cheap plans absorb "a lot of basic IT support" that larger customers handle in-house; low-value customers often lack contingency plans and file more tickets (Patrick McKenzie, ~January 2018) — [thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/955651276699721728.html).
- Anecdote on reducing churn: a setup fee made "The cancellation rate goes to near zero" for a productized website service — [SFTRFOU ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel).
- Customer-count arithmetic for $100M ARR (Christoph Janz): 100 whales at $1M, 1,000 elephants at $100k, 10,000 deer at $10k, 100,000 rabbits at $1k, 1M mice at $100 (flies at $10 dropped in the 2019 revision); "outbound sales doesn't work if your ARPA isn't large enough"; "most customer acquisition channels are either scalable or profitable but not both at the same time" — [Point Nine, 6 April 2019](https://writing.pointnine.com/p/five-years-later-five-ways-to-build).
- Sales economics (Tomasz Tunguz, 21 July 2016): at a $500 ACV and a $500k quota a rep must close 1,000 customers a year (4.2 per working day), "stratospheric - and unrealistic"; many startups use sales teams for accounts at "$3k+" because "the velocities are sustainable and the quotas are attainable"; below the line, self-signup and scalable support models — [tomtunguz.com](https://tomtunguz.com/smallest-acv-to-justify-inside-sales-team/).
- Heuristic thresholds (vendor glossaries, no data shown): self-serve below $5K ACV, inside sales $5K–$50K, field sales $50K+ — [Komo](https://komo.ai/glossary/average-contract-value); a sales hire does not pencil out below ~$5K and a product-only motion leaves revenue on the table above ~$25K (snippet) — [Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/annual-contract-value-benchmarks-seed-2026).
- AI-native retention by price (2025): GRR 70%/NRR 85% above $250/month, 45%/61% at $50–$249, 23%/32% below $50 — [ChartMogul AI churn wave](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-retention-the-ai-churn-wave/).
- Buyers' churn preference: strong SaaS retention is "typically under 10%" and buyers reportedly favour churn "below 6%" (no source given; period unstated) — [Flippa SaaS multiples](https://flippa.com/blog/saas-multiples/).

### Inferences
- **Customers needed for ≈10k/month net** (assumes a 70% margin, so ≈$14.3k MRR; the new customers needed each month just to replace churn = customers × monthly logo churn):

| ARPA ($/month) | Customers for $14.3k MRR | New/month at 3% churn | at 5% | at 8% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | ~1,430 | ~43 | ~71 | ~114 |
| 29 | ~490 | ~15 | ~25 | ~39 |
| 49 | ~290 | ~9 | ~15 | ~23 |
| 99 | ~145 | ~4.3 | ~7.2 | ~11.5 |
| 299 | ~48 | ~1.4 | ~2.4 | ~3.8 |
| 999 | ~14 | ~0.4 | ~0.7 | ~1.1 |

- Churn grounding: ChartMogul's top-quartile 63.1% annual retention at ARPA < $10 equals ≈3.8% monthly churn for the best quartile, so the median low-price product sits above that; at ARPA > $500 the top-quartile 85.7% annual retention equals ≈1.3% monthly. MicroConf's 5.5–11% averages put a typical indie product in the 5–8% columns.
- Reading the table: below ~$50/month a part-time founder must win 15–70 new paying customers every month forever just to stay at 10k, which requires a scalable channel (SEO, a marketplace, paid ads) and creates the heaviest support load per dollar (McKenzie). At $100–$300/month the treadmill drops to a few customers a month, which word of mouth, SEO, founder-led outreach or a niche community can sustain.
- Thresholds: ~$1k–$3k ACV (≈$80–$250/month) is the upper end where pure self-serve is natural; ~$3k–$5k+ ACV is where a human sales touch starts to pay (Tunguz, Komo, Causo). A part-time founder can sell $300–$1,000/month deals personally, but each such deal brings demos, procurement questions and sometimes security reviews (see section 4), so 15–50 customers at that price trade a churn treadmill for sales hours.
- Productized-service arithmetic: at $2,000–$5,000 per client per month, 10k net needs ~5–15 clients depending on delivery cost; if the founder delivers personally the margin is high but hours scale with clients, and if delivery is delegated, agency-like margins (13–30%) push the client count up 3–7x.
- Annual plans: ChartMogul reports median NRR 10–20 points higher on annual than monthly plans ([AI churn wave report](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-retention-the-ai-churn-wave/)), so annual billing at mid prices is the cheapest retention lever available to a small product.

### Gaps
- No dataset quantifies support tickets per customer by price tier; the evidence is practitioner experience (McKenzie) and one anecdote (Casel).
- The ACV thresholds come from a 2016 analysis of 13 public companies and from vendor glossaries without method; no study of thresholds for micro-SaaS was found.
- MicroConf's churn averages lack a stated period and distribution; medians were not available.

## 4. Low-hassle versus high-hassle business archetypes for a part-time founder

### Takeaway
The founder and market reports converge on a ranking: the lowest operating hassle is a mid-priced B2B niche product (or tightly scoped productized service) that owns its customer relationships and needs no single platform's permission. The highest is low-priced consumer subscriptions (churn treadmill), anything built on one platform's API or one search engine's traffic (Apollo, Tweetbot, GummySearch, HouseFresh), enterprise or regulated software (compliance and sales cycles), two-sided marketplaces and physical goods. Acquisition marketplaces price the same risks: content sites and marketplaces sell at the lowest profit multiples and owner-dependent businesses are discounted. No systematic survey of hours, support volume or on-call duty by archetype exists; the ranking rests on case reports, retention data and market pricing.

### Cited Findings
- Platform dependence: Apollo, a third-party Reddit client, shut down on 30 June 2023 after Reddit's API pricing ($12,000 per 50 million requests), which its developer estimated at about $20M a year for his usage; Tweetbot had already stopped after Twitter introduced API fees (snippets) — [MacRumors](https://macrumors.com/2023/06/08/apollo-shutting-down-june-30); [Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/135875/apollo-reddit-app-shutdown-api).
- Platform dependence, founder's own words: the GummySearch founder described "looking over your shoulder every day, wondering if tomorrow you get the axe" before closing over Reddit API licensing (one-person business, ~4 years) — [GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/final-chapter).
- Search dependence (content treadmill): HouseFresh, a small independent review site, lost 91% of its Google traffic, attributed to the March 2024 core update; daily Google visitors fell from about 4,000 to 200 from October 2023, as affiliate pages from large publishers outranked it (snippet) — [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/review-site-google-traffic-affiliate-seo-content-440143). Its founder later described a recovery owed to "hard work and a lot of luck" (snippet) — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/housefresh-achieves-notable-traffic-recovery-after-google-algorithm-impacts-2/).
- Market pricing of hassle and risk (Flippa closed deals, indicative): small owner-run SaaS ~2.5x–4.5x profit, top SaaS cohort up to 6.13x, stronger e-commerce cohorts ~3.98x, content and publishing ~2.32x, marketplaces ~2.02x; discounts for traffic "almost all search-driven", exposure "to algorithm swings and AI-driven search", dependence on one paid channel, one customer holding a large share of revenue, marketplace users taking deals off-platform, and a founder who personally handles sales, product and key accounts — [Flippa valuation multiples, updated 11 August 2026](https://flippa.com/blog/digital-business-valuation-multiples/).
- Buyers' scrutiny: "how much time the founder spends in the business", churn, documented SOPs, clean financials — [Acquire.com webinar recap, March 2026](https://blog.acquire.com/acquisition-multiples-report-2025-findings-webinar-recap/).
- Consumer subscriptions: median NRR 49% for B2C SaaS versus 82% for B2B SaaS (2025, ≥ $250k ARR) — [ChartMogul AI churn wave](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-retention-the-ai-churn-wave/); only 5% of B2C businesses reach customer retention above 85% — [ChartMogul Retention Report](https://chartmogul.com/saas-retention-report/). Consumer and government targets grew slowest in MicroConf's 2024 sample (~5% and ~1.75% average monthly growth) — [SFTRFOU ep. 721](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-721-7-key-takeaways-from-the-2024-state-of-independent-saas-report).
- Enterprise and regulated software: enterprise-targeting indie SaaS grew fastest (~26% average monthly) — [SFTRFOU ep. 721](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-721-7-key-takeaways-from-the-2024-state-of-independent-saas-report); a first-year SOC 2 programme costs roughly $20K–$40K for companies under 50 staff, with year-one totals quoted from about $25K to $100K+ and $15K–$40K a year of upkeep (vendor estimates, snippets) — [Hicomply, October 2025](https://hicomply.com/blog/soc-2-cost-how-much-does-it-really-cost-in-2025); [Drata](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/cost); [Agency](https://blog.getagency.com/articles/soc-2-audit-cost-for-startups).
- Support: small accounts generate disproportionately more tickets per account and per dollar (practitioner experience) — [Patrick McKenzie thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/955651276699721728.html).
- Services: consulting creep and the founder bottleneck are the named failure modes; documentation takes up to three times as long as doing the task once — [SFTRFOU ep. 208](https://startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-208-how-to-productize-your-service-with-brian-casel).
- Side-project stage: Walling's stair-step recommends one-time-sale add-ons in marketplaces (WordPress, Shopify) for the side-project stage because the marketplace supplies discovery (snippet) — [MicroConf repost](https://microconf.com/latest/the-stairstep-approach-to-bootstrapping).
- Part-time effect: full-time-led indie SaaS grow 30% faster than part-time-led ones — [MicroConf $0–10K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr).
- Paid acquisition hassle: 29% of indie founders running ads need 7+ months to see a return or cannot tell — [MicroConf $10K–100K ARR page](https://microconf.com/founders/10k-100k-arr).

### Inferences
- **Archetype ranking for a part-time operator** (synthesis; L = lower hassle, H = higher):

| Archetype | Hassle | Evidence-based reasons |
|---|---|---|
| B2B niche SaaS, self-serve, $50–$300/month, annual plans offered | L | Better retention above $25–$50 ARPA; fewer tickets per dollar; buyers pay 2.5–4.5x profit; slow growth is the cost |
| Productized service with fixed scope, setup fee, documented SOPs | L–M | Revenue before software; churn cut by setup fees (anecdote); hours scale with clients unless delegated |
| One-time-sale add-on in a marketplace (plugin, template, app) | L–M | Discovery supplied by the marketplace; but platform-policy and API risk (Apollo/Tweetbot pattern) |
| Low-price B2C or prosumer subscription (< $25/month), incl. AI apps | H | B2C NRR 49%; AI apps < $50 GRR 23%; constant acquisition; support per dollar highest |
| Content, SEO or affiliate site | H | Algorithm and AI-search exposure (HouseFresh); lowest multiples (~2.3x) |
| Tool built on one platform's data API (Reddit, X, Google) | H | Closure risk outside your control (GummySearch, Apollo) |
| Two-sided marketplace | H | Lowest multiples (~2.0x); disintermediation; two audiences to acquire |
| E-commerce / physical goods | H | Working capital, inventory and ad-spend dependence (Flippa drivers); multiples ~4x only for stronger cohorts |
| Enterprise or regulated SaaS | H for part-timers | Fastest growth when it works, but SOC 2-type costs, sales-led ACV thresholds and long cycles |

- For a part-time founder, the sustainable pattern in these sources is a business that (1) owns its customer list and channel (email, community, word of mouth, SEO on owned content rather than a single platform), (2) charges enough per customer that a few new customers a month cover churn, and (3) can be documented so the founder's hours are not the product. These are also the features buyers pay more for, which makes the business saleable later.

### Gaps
- No systematic dataset on founder hours, support volume or on-call duty by business type was found; MicroConf's accessible pages do not report hours per week or burnout.
- No primary data was found on enterprise sales-cycle length for micro-SaaS, or on how many part-time founders sustain each archetype.
- Evidence on two-sided marketplaces and physical goods for solo founders is limited to marketplace multiples and broker commentary.

## 5. Buying versus building: 2025–2026 multiples for micro-SaaS, deal sizes for 5–15k/month profit, the main risks, and how often buyers keep the revenue

### Takeaway
Small profitable SaaS sold in 2025–2026 at about 3–5x annual profit: Acquire.com's closed-deal median was 3.9x in both 2024 and 2025 (3.7x average for deals under $100k net income), Flippa's owner-run SaaS range was 2.5–4.5x in H1 2026, and Empire Flippers' "typical" listing across categories is 2.3x annual net profit. A business earning 5–15k/month therefore costs roughly $140k–$800k, mostly $220k–$700k for SaaS. Buying removes the zero-to-revenue risk, which is where most built businesses die, but brings capital, debt and inherited risks: churn, founder dependence, platform concentration and "AI shifts". No marketplace publishes how often buyers keep the revenue after purchase; the nearest data (search funds, much larger deals) shows about 31% of acquisitions losing money.

### Cited Findings

**Multiples (profit-based unless stated)**
- Acquire.com (report published 11 February 2026, covering 2025): SaaS median profit multiple 3.9x in 2024 and in 2025 (flat); average "in the low-to-mid 4x range"; average time on market 81 days; segment typically enterprise value below $10M; public SaaS revenue multiples fell from ~17x (2022) to ~7x (2023–24) to ~5.5x (end 2025) while Acquire prices "proved more resilient"; data is "anonymized, customer-provided" and no sample size is given — [Acquire.com Jan 2026 report](https://blog.acquire.com/acquire-com-biannual-acquisition-multiples-report-jan-2026/).
- Acquire.com webinar recap (20 March 2026, "136+ deals"): businesses under $100K net income averaged 3.7x, $100K–$1M averaged 3.9x; overall "between 3 and 5x net income"; listings at fair market value engage "roughly 60% of the active buyer pool", 5–10% above market cuts interest to "around 30%"; time on market 80–90 days, well-priced deals close in 30 days or fewer; earnout risks include "Market disruptions, AI shifts, or a bad quarter post-close"; buyers rank profit margin, then growth, then operating history ("three or more years being a strong signal") — [Acquire.com webinar recap](https://blog.acquire.com/acquisition-multiples-report-2025-findings-webinar-recap/).
- Acquire.com, February 2023 (H2 2022 deals): SaaS 2–3x revenue or ~5x profit (snippet) — [Acquire.com Feb 2023 report](https://blog.acquire.com/acquire-com-biannual-acquisition-multiples-report-feb-2023/).
- Flippa (closed deals, H1 2026; no sample sizes): small owner-run SaaS ~2.5x–4.5x profit; top SaaS cohort up to 6.13x; larger private SaaS ~3x–10x ARR. All digital businesses by deal size: $10K–$100K average 2.24x (top quartile 5.96x), $100K–$250K 1.85x (3.82x), $250K–$1M 1.82x (2.84x), $1M+ 2.43x+ (5.42x); $100K–$1M is a "Valuation Compression Zone" where buyers commit more money with less institutional backing — [Flippa valuation multiples](https://flippa.com/blog/digital-business-valuation-multiples/).
- Flippa SaaS page (updated 20 February 2026): a Flippa broker says "Typically below ARR of $1M, the business is owner-operated" and buyers "will pay a multiple on profit generated, typically anywhere from 2x–4x"; by category (past 18 months): marketing tools 5.9x profit, education 4.1x, health 3.9x, internet and security 3.4x, CRM 2.6x; five highlighted SaaS sales at $496K–$1.35M with 2.8x–5.8x profit multiples; Flippa says it had facilitated 795 SaaS sales by end 2024 — [Flippa SaaS multiples](https://flippa.com/blog/saas-multiples/). Flippa's 2025 insights list SaaS at a 2.7x average and 5.8x top quartile, basis not stated (snippet) — [Flippa 2025 insights](https://flippa.com/blog/2025-online-business-ma-insights-from-flippa/).
- Empire Flippers (all multiples shown as annual net profit since 8 September 2026): category averages from its 2026 State of the Industry report: distressed 1.1x, typical 2.3x, premium 2.6x, 7-figure premium 3.4x; "most small businesses trade between 1x and 5x their annual SDE or EBITDA"; 2,681+ completed deals, $600M+ sold — [Empire Flippers](https://empireflippers.com/switching-to-annual-multiples/).
- FE International: small businesses typically 2x–4x SDE; SaaS under about $5M is usually priced off SDE when growth is modest and there is no management layer (snippets) — [FE International valuation, 2025](https://feinternational.com/blog/how-much-business-worth-valuation-2025); [FE SaaS metrics](https://www.feinternational.com/blog/saas-metrics-value-saas-business).
- Asking prices (not closed deals): 275 SaaS-category listings on Acquire.com had medians of 2.30x revenue and 3.60x profit; the full 615-listing set ~2.0x revenue and 3.4x profit (June 2026, snippet) — [BigIdeasDB PDF](https://nlxukgfwdfiytdptdwxp.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/landing-uploads/saas-reports/state-of-saas-acquisitions-2026.pdf). A conflicting blog average of 10.7x trailing profit across 600+ listings is internally inconsistent (its own example implies ~4.6x) (snippet) — [Beancount.io, July 2026](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/11/bootstrapped-saas-valuation-multiples-2026-acquire-com-indie-founders-guide).
- MicroConf sellers: 60% of exits at 1x–3x forward-looking ARR (revenue basis, sellers in the 2024 survey) — [MicroConf by-stage page](https://microconf.com/by-stage).
- SaaS Capital: predicted valuation multiple 4.8x for bootstrapped versus 5.3x for equity-backed companies (2025; basis not stated in the excerpt, likely revenue for $3M+ ARR firms) — [SaaS Capital bootstrapped benchmarks](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/benchmarking-metrics-for-bootstrapped-saas-companies/).

**Risks and post-acquisition outcomes**
- Buyers inherit the previous owner's churn and technical debt (practitioner guide) — [DEV Community acquisition guide](https://dev.to/alexcloudstar/how-to-buy-a-micro-saas-a-practical-acquisition-guide-43b4).
- Seller financing and earnouts "increase the risk of not seeing the full amount" for sellers, and competitive deals favour cash-heavy structures — [Acquire.com webinar recap](https://blog.acquire.com/acquisition-multiples-report-2025-findings-webinar-recap/).
- Anecdotes: a multi-deal micro-SaaS buyer reports revenue up over 47% year on year at his fourth acquisition — [Justin Butlion's Substack](https://justinbutlion.substack.com/p/my-4th-saas-acquisition); the same buyer describes cash flow tightened by payments owed to former owners and the hidden load of running several small businesses (snippet) — [Buying Online Businesses podcast](https://omny.fm/shows/buying-online-businesses-podcast/how-to-win-micro-saas-acquisitions-with-seller-financing-even-against-higher-offers-with-justin-butlion).
- Search funds (Stanford GSB 2024 study; larger, lower-middle-market deals, US and Canada): aggregate IRR 35.1% and ROI 4.5x; among acquired companies roughly 31% returned a loss (20.5% partial, 10.5% total); a cumulative 58% of funds since 1996 completed an acquisition (snippets; report not opened; sample cited as 681 funds on one page and 500+ on another) — [Searchfunder summary](https://searchfunder.com/post/stanford-2024-search-fund-study-highlights); [Stanford GSB highlights](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/alumni/career-resources/video-library/highlights-2024-search-fund-study).

**Build-side comparison (from section 2)**
- Median time to $1M ARR 2y9m for survivors; 13% of startups reach $10M ARR within a decade — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-report/); largest indie MRR bucket under $1k MRR and ~30% flat or shrinking — [MicroConf pages](https://microconf.com/founders/0-10k-arr); 34.7% of US establishments survive ten years — [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm).

### Inferences
- **Indicative purchase prices for 5–15k/month of profit** (annual profit × multiple):

| Monthly profit (annual) | EF typical 2.3x | Flippa owner-run SaaS 2.5–4.5x | Acquire average by size (3.7x < $100k; 3.9x $100k–$1M) | Asking-price median 3.6x |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5k (60k) | ~$138k | ~$150k–$270k | ~$222k | ~$216k |
| 10k (120k) | ~$276k | ~$300k–$540k | ~$468k | ~$432k |
| 15k (180k) | ~$414k | ~$450k–$810k | ~$702k | ~$648k |

- At 3.9x a buyer pays about 47 months of profit, before tax and debt service and before paying anyone to run the business; at 2.3x about 28 months. Since "profit" in these listings often means owner's earnings (SDE), a part-time buyer who must hire an operator receives less than the headline figure.
- Build versus buy for a part-time founder: building costs little cash but carries the base rates above (most indie products never reach $1k MRR; a top-5–10% outcome is needed for 10k/month); buying costs $200k–$700k (often partly seller-financed) and replaces market risk with execution and retention risk. A plausible hybrid matching the stair-step logic is to buy a small (sub-$100k) business in a niche the factory already researched, or to build until a small validated product exists and then add acquisitions in the same niche.
- Deals in the $100k–$1M range are the "compression zone" (lower multiples, fewer institutional buyers), which favours buyers of 5–15k/month businesses but also signals higher perceived risk in that size band.
- Because buyers explicitly price "AI shifts" into earnout risk, small SaaS whose core function a general AI assistant could replicate is likely to trade at the low end of the ranges or require earnouts.

### Gaps
- No marketplace or study reports how often micro-SaaS buyers keep or grow revenue after purchase (retention of acquired revenue at 12–24 months); only anecdotes and search-fund data on much larger deals were found.
- Acquire.com, Flippa and Empire Flippers publish no sample sizes for most multiples, and their bases differ (net income, SDE, net profit); Empire Flippers' SaaS-specific average was not found in the material accessible here.
- Typical seller-financing shares and earnout terms for micro-SaaS deals were not quantified in any source found.

## 6. What changed in 2025–2026 with AI: wrapper saturation, service-as-software, vertical AI agents and distribution as the moat (evidence versus opinion)

### Takeaway
The evidence shows a split market: the top AI companies reach revenue milestones faster than SaaS ever did (Stripe), while the typical AI product, especially a low-priced one, retains customers far worse than SaaS (ChartMogul: 40% median GRR for AI-native companies, 23% below $50/month; RevenueCat: AI apps churn about 30% faster). Price is the dividing line: AI-native products above $250/month retain like B2B SaaS. "Service-as-software", vertical AI agents being "10x bigger than SaaS" and "distribution is the moat" are investor and founder opinions without outcome data, though they are consistent with the retention-by-price evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Evidence (measured data)**
- AI-native retention (ChartMogul with Kyle Poyar, 10 December 2025; 3,500 companies incl. ~200 AI-native; cohort ≥ $250k ARR): AI-native NRR 48% and GRR 40% versus median NRR 82% for B2B SaaS and 49% for B2C SaaS; AI GRR rose from 27% in January to 40% in September 2025 (the author suspects early "AI tourists" left, unmeasured); by price: above $250/month GRR 70%/NRR 85% ("essentially the same" as B2B SaaS), $50–$249 45%/61%, below $50 23%/32%; among low-retention companies, three times as many are shrinking as growing quickly; median NRR 10–20 points higher on annual than monthly plans — [ChartMogul AI churn wave](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-retention-the-ai-churn-wave/).
- AI consumer apps (RevenueCat, 18 August 2026; 3,519 AI apps, subscriptions started July 2024–June 2025, 50M+ subscriptions): one-year retention on monthly plans 6.1% for AI apps versus 9.5% for non-AI apps, on annual plans 21.1% versus 30.7%; "AI apps churn at 30% higher rates"; median first-year revenue per payer 41% higher ($30.16 versus $21.37); top-tier AI apps retain 13.9% of subscriptions after a year versus 1.4% for the bottom tier; apps launched 2024 onward skew to the low-retention tier; AI-powered apps are 25% of subscription apps — [RevenueCat AI app retention study](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/ai-app-retention-study).
- Growth of the top AI companies (Stripe's own data): the top 100 AI companies by revenue reached $5M annualized revenue in 24 months (2024) versus 37 months for the top 100 SaaS companies in 2018 — [TechCrunch on Stripe's 2024 letter, 27 February 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/27/stripe-ceo-says-ai-startups-are-growing-faster-than-saas-ever-did-and-calling-them-wrappers-misses-the-point); the number of companies reaching $10M ARR within three months of creation doubled in 2025 versus 2024 (no absolute count) — [Stripe 2025 update](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-2025-update).
- Faster time to first revenue: 20% of Stripe Atlas startups charged a first customer within 30 days in 2025 versus 8% in 2020 — [Stripe 2025 update](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-2025-update).
- A thin-wrapper case (reported): Jasper's annualized revenue reached ~$80M in 2022; after ChatGPT, subscribers paying ~$50/month reportedly moved to free or $20 plans, the 2023 outlook was cut by at least 30%, and staff were laid off in July 2023, under nine months after a $125M raise (snippets; The Information's original not seen) — [Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/jasper-cuts-internal-valuation-as-ai-growth-slows/); [Voicebot.ai](https://voicebot.ai/2023/07/17/jasper-ai-laying-off-staff-9-months-after-125m-raise/).
- Buyers price AI risk: "AI shifts" listed as a post-close earnout risk — [Acquire.com webinar recap](https://blog.acquire.com/acquisition-multiples-report-2025-findings-webinar-recap/); content sites discounted for exposure to "AI-driven search" — [Flippa](https://flippa.com/blog/digital-business-valuation-multiples/).
- Data access tightened for AI-era research tools: GummySearch closed after failing to license Reddit's Data API (2025) — [GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/final-chapter).

**Opinion and forecasts (no outcome data)**
- Service-as-software (Foundation Capital, VC thesis first published 2024; the fetched page carries a 2026 date): vendors move from selling tools to owning outcomes, with AI agents doing the work, priced on results ("charge based on the number of qualified opportunities or signed customers their software delivers"); the "$4.6 trillion" figure sums salary pools and Gartner IT/BPO services spending and is framed by the firm as "a $4.6 trillion-dollar question"; examples are mostly its portfolio companies, and humans still "design the workflows" — [Foundation Capital](https://foundationcapital.com/ai-service-as-software/).
- Vertical AI agents: YC partners (Lightcone podcast, 22 November 2024) argued vertical AI agents "could be 10X bigger than SaaS", which Jared Friedman framed as "very possible", not certain (snippet) — [Lightcone episode](https://podcast.app/vertical-ai-agents-could-be-10x-bigger-than-saas-e365591200); [Virtualization Review summary](https://virtualizationreview.com/Articles/2024/12/05/The-Future-of-AI-Follow-the-Money.aspx).
- Wrappers: the Collisons argued critics calling AI startups "LLM wrappers" are "missing the point", since AI is going vertical as SaaS did and "the contextual, data, and workflow integration will prove enduringly valuable" — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/27/stripe-ceo-says-ai-startups-are-growing-faster-than-saas-ever-did-and-calling-them-wrappers-misses-the-point).
- Distribution as the moat: essays argue that as building gets cheap, distribution becomes the scarce resource (snippets; the idea dates to an a16z essay by Alex Rampell in 2015) — [Unmitigated Risk](https://unmitigatedrisk.com/?p=1170); [SaaS Club on Tibo Louis-Lucas](https://saasclub.io/playbooks/tibo-louis-lucas-distribution-moat-opposite/); a counter-essay calls the slogan half right — [Kingy.ai](https://kingy.ai/news/your-product-isnt-the-moat-your-distribution-is/).

### Inferences
- For a bootstrapped founder, the data supports avoiding low-priced, general-purpose AI tools (below ~$50/month), where retention is a quarter of B2B SaaS levels and a free general assistant is the substitute (the Jasper pattern), and favouring AI that does a specific job for a business at $250+/month, which retains like ordinary B2B SaaS. That is the measurable core of the "service-as-software" and "vertical agent" opinions.
- Cheaper building (faster first revenue, more products launched) increases competition for attention, which is consistent with, but does not prove, the "distribution is the moat" view; for the factory it argues for weighting the "route to buyers" and owned channels at least as heavily as the product idea.
- Stripe's growth figures describe the top 100 AI companies, a selection of outliers; they are not base rates for an indie AI product, whose typical retention (ChartMogul, RevenueCat) is worse than SaaS.
- A productized service delivered with AI (selling the outcome at service prices with software-like margins) fits both the service-as-software thesis and the part-time constraints of section 4, but no data on its margins or retention was found.

### Gaps
- No dataset measures how many thin AI wrappers launched and failed in 2024–2026; saturation is inferred from retention data and individual cases.
- No outcome data exists yet for "service-as-software" or outcome-priced AI businesses at small scale; the sources are VC theses and portfolio examples.
- MicroConf's accessible 2024 material has no AI-specific findings, and no 2025–2026 indie-founder survey on AI products was retrieved.
